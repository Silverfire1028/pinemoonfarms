## [Nature's Weather Predictors and How To Read Them](https://hotmesstohomestead.wordpress.com/2020/10/10/natures-weather-predictors-and-how-to-read-them/)

Hi! Today is small talk day. Today, we're going to talk about the weather.

Nature has so many ways to tell us when winter is approaching. Living on a farm, you learn to listen to them, loud or subtle. Unfortunately, these old and tried methods and what I've learned over the years are becoming less and less accurate as the climate continues to change. Sources like the Old Farmers Almanac are general at best, and inaccurate at worse, and usually cover wide areas and dismisses microclimate. Weather stations rarely provide predictions past a few weeks [(it's really hard)](https://science.howstuffworks.com/nature/climate-weather/atmospheric/scientists-predict-weather.htm), and anything broader covers a wide region, and frequently dismisses microclimate.

A **[microclimate](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microclimate#:~:text=A microclimate (or micro-climate,sometimes with a substantial one.)** is a local set of atmospheric conditions that differ from those in the surrounding areas, often with a slight difference but sometimes with a substantial one. Where I live, rain frequently curves around us because (I assume) we live at the top of a, rather large, hill. I can stand on my front porch and watch a rain front move north and the cloud bank shift to mirror the curve in the land. Because of this, my climate is much different than the next city over. My weather is frequently drastically different than even a couple miles away.

Because of how complex the weather and climate is, I strongly encourage you to get to know your own climate and weather, and not rely on a forecast as your sole source for weather predictions. Learning the pattern that your land goes though is invaluable, and creates an addicting game of spot the difference everyday between you and nature.

Start observing the small places around your home, like the bases of trees, under bushes, the tips of limbs everyday. Try to make mental note of the changes day to day, or keep a log if you don't think you'll remember. These places are the first to change, and will almost always be the same from year to year. For example, I have a cluster of tulips that always emerge early, right on the edge of my property. They tell me, every year, when spring is coming. The tree tips will visibly turn color before the leaves emerge. In the fall, I watch for the seed heads and nuts to form, and for my horses coat to grow in, and they can tell me how fast winter will be here. During the summer, watch your native plants and the order that they bloom and go to seed. This is nature's timeline during the green months, when the progress of the season is almost invisible here, now. If you don't pay close attention, it seems to jump from winter to summer, then summer to winter, with a brief show of color in the fall. By paying closer attention, I have learned to appreciate the seasons change and feel much more at harmony with my farm than in years past.

Something I have specifically noticed about my area for the past few years, is that spring weather closely matches winter weather for a given year. For example, a dry, hot spring means a dry, warm winter, a wet, hot spring means lots of rain over the winter, and a wet, cold spring means a lot of snow, etc. However, with [rising temperatures changing the jet stream](https://www.startribune.com/believe-it-or-not-global-warming-might-have-caused-the-arctic-blast/505252892/#:~:text=Arctic weather plunges into North America&text=But disturbances in the jet,air south into middle latitudes.), the polar vortex (a persistent, large scale, upper-level low pressure area, less than 620 miles, in diameter, that rotates counter-clockwise at the north pole) is dipping down more frequently, causing more and more “artic blasts”. Even though I am expecting a warm, wet winter, I expect this will cross over with the artic blasts and cause us to have more, but brief (a week or less), snow accumulation and flooding. I hope not, the snow makes everything harder around here, and everything floods… The point is, learn your local weather patterns, and not much can take you by surprise anymore, weather wise. Even a little bit of forewarning is valuable.

Living close to the land brings on it's own unique challenges, and believe me, there is no reason to make farming any harder. Knowing your weather is an essential skill to growing, farming, and any type of homestead, big or small, because it allows you to plan your farming season/birthing season, and allows you to plan ahead for weather challenges. For example, we have roof repair supplies set aside for the barns in case a panel blows loose and salt on hand for ice during the winter. Nothing can ruin your plans like unexpected bad weather, trust me.
